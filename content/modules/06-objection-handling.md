Objections are not failures. They are information. This module gives you the canonical handles for the nine objections you'll hear every day.

## Core Principles

1. **There is always an objection behind the objection.** The stated reason is almost never the real reason. Your job is to surface the real one.
2. **Never say "I totally understand."** It sounds like you've been trained to say it. Instead: acknowledge briefly, then ask the next question.
3. **Ask questions that surface their own reasoning.** Not to make them feel bad, but to expose the gap between their stated goal and their current hesitation.
4. **Three canonical framing questions:** "Does it not make sense to...?" "Is it not worth...?" "Are you opposed to...?" **Use these on repeat.**
5. **Proactively pre-empt.** Build the top three objection answers into your pitch before they come up. The objection that never gets raised is the easiest one to handle.

## The Objections and How to Handle Them

### 1. "I need to think about it."

> "What specifically do you need to think about? Because you already told me [restate their stated goal], and what I'm offering solves exactly that. Walk me through what's holding you back."

If they still stall: takeaway.

> "It sounds like maybe this isn't the right moment. We're at [tier X] right now — when we fill up, the next lead pays [higher price]. If you change your mind, text me. I'd love to help but only if you're ready."

### 2. "It's more expensive than I thought."

> "Compared to what? Walk me through what you were expecting. [Let them answer.] Okay — so a plane ticket to Hawaii is $600. Two dinners out is $200. What I'm asking is [$X] a month for something that hits every goal you just told me about. Is that not worth it?"

**Alternative** (reframe the cost against their stated emotional weight):

> "You told me you want community, strength, and better sleep. If I asked you for $29 for a piece of paper, that's expensive. If I asked you for $29 a month for community, muscle, body composition, cardiovascular health, and better sleep — is that not worth it?"

### 3. "I need to check with my spouse."

> "Completely hear you — I check with mine too. However: what if they said no? What if they said they don't support you doing something for yourself?"

The silence after this question is doing the work. **Do not rescue them from it.**

When they respond — usually with "well, usually when it's financial I talk to him" — you've now surfaced the real objection (money). Handle that one.

**Alternative spouse handles:**

- **Reciprocity reframe:** "Does he ask before grabbing a beer with Chad? Does he ask what you think of every shirt before buying it?"
- **Referral stack:** "Bring him too — he gets the same founder rate and you both get $25 off."
- **Downshift to 4-session:** "Let's lock you in on the four-session — way easier to explain, and you're still a founder."

### 4. "I want to try it first."

> "I hear you. The founder membership IS the trial — you're charged once, you have a grace period to get a full refund if you don't love it, and the rate is lower than what anyone will ever pay after opening. So if you walk into that first class and hate it, you're out. Zero risk. That's as close to 'try it first' as we offer — and it comes with a rate lock nobody off the street will get."

### 5. "I'm already doing [Competitor]."

> "Okay — so you already like this kind of training. Then I'm curious why you filled out our form. Because people don't fill out forms for places they're fully happy with. What's missing at [Competitor]?"

Once they tell you what's missing, you've got your pitch. Then layer social proof:

> "Honestly, I've talked to dozens of [Competitor] customers who said the exact same thing. You're not alone. And that's exactly why they made the switch."

> **On disparaging competitors:** Don't bash the competitor directly. Attribute criticism to the crowd: "I've been hearing…" or "I've had people tell me…" You become the neutral messenger relaying what their peers are saying. Lower defenses, same effect.

### 6. "You're not open yet."

> "Right — and that's exactly why you get this rate. After we open, this membership goes up [X%]. You're not charged a penny until we open. If you walk in on day one and hate it, you get a full refund. So there is genuinely zero risk to locking in now versus paying [higher price] later."

People who raise the opening-date objection are almost always easy to close — they're telling you the only thing holding them back is a detail you can eliminate in one sentence.

### 7. "Can you call me back?"

> "Sure, no problem. Before I do — I don't want to waste your time or mine. If you give me 90 seconds right now, I can see if this is even a fit. If it's not, I won't call you again. Fair?"

The structure: **accept the callback in word, but negotiate 90 seconds now in deed.** Most leads will give you the 90 seconds.

### 8. Dead air after a text.

> **"Hey [Name] — how do you want to proceed?"**

That's the whole text. Direct. Short. Puts the decision in their hands without pushing. It gets responses at roughly **double** the rate of a "just checking in" text.

### 9. "I want to talk to a manager" / hostile tone.

Do not let them push your respect down. Takeaway with professionalism:

> "It sounds like maybe this isn't the right moment. We're happy to have you as a customer when we open — at that point the price is [higher price]. If anything changes and you want to save money before we open, feel free to reach out. I hope you have a great rest of your day."

The tone: **"I'm fine either way."** Not desperate, not sarcastic. It re-inserts urgency without begging.

## Takeaway Ladder

When a lead resists more than once, **escalate your takeaway in stages** rather than fighting on the same objection:

1. **Light takeaway:** restate the upside, acknowledge the hesitation, ask one last question.
2. **Medium takeaway:** "It sounds like maybe this isn't the right fit right now. That's okay — we'll be here when you're ready. Fair?"
3. **Walk-away:** "I appreciate your time. Have a great day." And mean it.

The walk-away is not a bluff. Sometimes they call back. When they do, you're in the strongest possible position.

## Fresh Voice Close

When you've exhausted your handles and the lead is genuinely close but not closing, hand them to your manager or another rep.

> "You know what — let me grab my manager real quick. She's great at these. One second."

A fresh voice can land a handle yours won't, because you've become adversarial in the lead's head. **Ego is cheap. Sales are not.**

## What Not to Say

- **"I totally understand."** Sounds scripted. Replace with one sincere word ("Gotcha") then a question.
- **"Is now a good time?"** Trained brush-off trigger.
- **"I'll email you info."** You've just lost the sale. Never promise an email as a substitute for a close.
- **"Take your time!"** You just told them speed doesn't matter. Speed always matters.
- **"Look, I'm just trying to help you out here."** Desperate. They can hear it. Replace with silence.
